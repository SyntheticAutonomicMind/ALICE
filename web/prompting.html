Ultimate Prompt Engineering Guide

Master the art of crafting effective prompts for Stable Diffusion and MiniMax Music 3 using proven techniques and professional best practices.

Core Principles: The Three-Act Prompt Structure

All successful prompts follow a narrative structure that models parse sequentially with descending attention weight.

High-Value Start (Act I)

Purpose: Set artistic medium, quality level, and primary composition

Position: Absolute beginning of prompt

Effect: Strongest influence on style and technical execution

The first 10-15 tokens establish the fundamental style and quality. The model gives these concepts the most weight.

Description Middle (Act II)

Purpose: Define characters, actions, environments, and relationships

Position: Middle section after style tags

Effect: Creates coherence and narrative depth

The core narrative containing subject, action, and scene details. This is where specificity and storytelling happen.

High-Value End (Act III)

Purpose: Specify lighting, perspective, camera effects, and finishing touches

Position: Final section before negative prompt

Effect: Polishes the image and solidifies technical execution

Technical and atmospheric refinements that lock in the final look.

Bracketing Syntax for Attention Control

Use parentheses to amplify specific elements:

  • (word) = 1.1x emphasis
  • ((word)) = 1.21x emphasis
  • (((word))) = 1.33x emphasis
  • {word} = stylistic grouping
  • [word] = 0.9x de-emphasis

Stable Diffusion 1.5 / 2.1

Character: Requires explicit, structured prompting with strong quality tags. Benefits from specific artistic references and concrete details.

Optimal Schema Structure

{QUALITY_TAGS}, {ART_MEDIUM}, {COMPOSITION}, (({SUBJECT})), {ACTION}, in {SCENE}, wearing {CLOTHING}, {FEATURES}, {COLOR_PALETTE}, {LIGHTING}, {TECHNICAL_TAGS}

Complete Example Prompt

masterpiece, best quality, digital painting, medium shot, ((elderly botanist)), carefully pruning exotic orchids, in a Victorian-era glass greenhouse, wearing tweed vest with leather elbow patches, wire-rimmed glasses, weathered hands, intelligent gaze, muted green and terracotta color scheme, dappled sunlight through glass panes, volumetric fog, intricate details, 8k resolution, trending on ArtStation

Development Strategy

  1. Start with Maximum Quality: Always begin with masterpiece, best quality plus your primary art medium
  2. Establish Composition Immediately: Follow with shot type (close-up, medium shot, wide shot) before introducing subject
  3. Emphasize Core Subject: Use double parentheses around your main character or focal point
  4. Build Layered Scene: Connect action to environment using natural phrasing
  5. Add Texture Through Details: Include specific materials, textures, and small features
  6. Finish with Technical Polish: End with lighting, color mood, and resolution tags

SDXL (Stable Diffusion XL)

Character: Understands natural language better, excels at atmospheric coherence. Less dependent on rigid structure but benefits from scene-based storytelling.

Optimal Schema Structure

{STYLE_DESCRIPTOR} of {MAIN_SUBJECT}, {ACTION_PHRASE}, in {ENVIRONMENT}, {ATMOSPHERIC_DETAILS}. {CHARACTER_DESCRIPTION}, {VISUAL_ELEMENTS}, {COMPOSITION}, {ARTISTIC_INFLUENCES}

Complete Example Prompt

A cinematic photograph of a marine biologist, releasing a rehabilitated sea turtle at sunrise, in crystal-clear tropical shallows. She wears a practical wetsuit, sun-bleached hair tied back, expression of quiet triumph, gentle hands guiding the turtle. Sunlight penetrates the water creating god rays, schools of small fish in background, sand stirring gently. Development Strategy

  1. Start with Complete Sentence: Frame as "A [style] of [subject], [action], in [environment]"
  2. Use Periods for Sections: SDXL understands paragraph-like structure with periods as soft breaks
  3. Focus on Atmosphere: Describe mood, light quality, and environmental interactions
  4. Include Sensory Details: Mention textures, temperatures, and movement
  5. Reference Specific Styles: Use known photographers, publications, or art movements
  6. End with Emotional Quality: Conclude with the feeling or narrative you want to convey

SDXL + Pony Diffusion V6

Character: Specialized for human subjects and dynamic scenes. Exceptional at anatomy, expressions, and complex interactions. Requires explicit relationship and action descriptions.

Optimal Schema Structure

{CINEMATIC_STYLE}, {LIGHTING}, {SHOT_TYPE}, {ANGLE}, (({SCENE_CONTEXT})), (({PRIMARY_ACTION})), ((({CHARACTER}))), {APPEARANCE}. {{FEATURES}}, (({SECONDARY_ACTION})), {INTERACTION_DETAILS}: {PERSPECTIVE}, {TECHNICAL_EXECUTION}

Complete Example Prompt

film still, dramatic chiaroscuro lighting, full body shot, low angle, ((in a grand library with towering oak bookshelves)), ((two rival academics fiercely debating over an ancient manuscript)), (((a brilliant but disheveled professor in his 40s))), wearing a rumpled corduroy jacket with leather patches. He has intense dark eyes, tousled salt-and-pepper hair, gesturing emphatically with one hand, ((the other hand gripping the edge of the reading table)), scattered papers and open books around them: Dutch angle perspective, deep focus, 35mm film grain, high contrast

Development Strategy

  1. Lead with Cinematic Language: Begin with film/photography terms to establish realism
  2. Establish Spatial Context Early: Use double parentheses for the environment immediately after shot specs
  3. Amplify Relationships: Put interactions in double parentheses, main character in triple
  4. Describe Sequential Actions: Use multiple action clauses to create narrative movement
  5. Group Physical Features: Use curly braces for facial/body description blocks
  6. End with Director's Perspective: Conclude with camera technique and post-processing

z-image-turbo

Character: Optimized for speed, requires concise, efficient prompting. Benefits from fewer but highly specific tokens. Less effective with complex scene descriptions.

Optimal Schema Structure

{STYLE}, {SUBJECT}, {ACTION}, {SCENE}, {KEY_DETAILS}, {QUALITY}

Complete Example Prompt

portrait photography, street musician playing violin, during golden hour, urban alley with graffiti walls, faded denim jacket, eyes closed in concentration, motion blur on bow, shallow depth of field, photorealistic, sharp focus

Development Strategy

  1. Be Extremely Concise: Limit to 30-50 tokens maximum
  2. Prioritize Visual Keywords: Use nouns and adjectives that directly translate to visual elements
  3. Skip Connective Language: Omit "in", "with", "and" when possible
  4. Focus on 3-4 Core Elements: Subject + action + scene + one key detail
  5. Use Simple Emphasis: Single parentheses only if absolutely necessary
  6. End with Quality Baseline: One or two quality/technical tags

Token Efficiency

~40 tokens optimal

Minimal Negative Prompt

cartoon, 3d, painting, blurry, deformed

Scene Development Framework

Phase 1: Conceptual Foundation

  1. Define the single core story moment
  2. Choose primary subject and their emotional state
  3. Select environment that enhances the narrative
  4. Determine time of day/lighting that matches mood

Phase 2: Layered Description

  1. First Layer: Primary subject + action + environment (basic sentence)
  2. Second Layer: Clothing + key physical features + props
  3. Third Layer: Secondary subjects/interactions + atmospheric details
  4. Fourth Layer: Camera perspective + lighting effects

Phase 3: Technical Refinement

  1. Add medium/style tags appropriate to your model
  2. Apply emphasis brackets to critical elements
  3. Include quality/resolution tags for final polish
  4. Test with minimal negative prompt first, then expand as needed

Phase 4: Iterative Testing

  1. Generate with core prompt only
  2. Add one detail layer at a time, regenerating each time
  3. Adjust emphasis based on what the model emphasizes
  4. Finalize negative prompt based on recurring artifacts

Advanced Techniques

Attention Migration

Move important elements earlier in the prompt to increase their influence. If a detail is being ignored, reposition it within the first 20 tokens.

Cross-Model Adaptation

To adapt a prompt between models:

  • SD 1.5 → SDXL: Convert fragments to complete sentences
  • SDXL → Pony: Add cinematic terms and anatomical specificity
  • Any → z-turbo: Remove 60% of tokens, keep only visual nouns

Troubleshooting Common Issues

Poor Anatomy

Solution: Add specific feature descriptions earlier in prompt

Ignored Details

Solution: Move detail earlier AND add parentheses

Style Inconsistency

Solution: Strengthen style tags at beginning AND end

Composition Issues

Solution: Specify shot type, angle, and perspective explicitly

Final Checklist Before Generation

  • Starts with style/quality tags
  • Includes specific shot type and composition
  • Main subject is clearly defined and emphasized
  • Action is dynamic and specific
  • Environment supports the narrative
  • Ends with technical/quality reinforcement
  • Token count appropriate for model
  • Negative prompt addresses model's specific weaknesses

Remember: Prompt engineering is iterative. The perfect prompt often requires 5-15 generations with small adjustments. Document what changes produce what effects to build your personal prompting style.

MiniMax Music 3

MiniMax Music 3 is an open-weights text-to-music model that generates complete, structurally coherent songs from a music description (the prompt field) and optional lyrics (the lyrics field). It uses a Qwen3-8B language model for long-range structure, flow-matching diffusion for audio synthesis, and a DAC vocoder. Generation supports up to 360 seconds (6 minutes) per clip.

ALICE API Mapping: The Music Description textarea on the Music page maps to the prompt parameter. The Lyrics textarea maps to the lyrics parameter. Leave lyrics empty for instrumental output.

  • Max duration: 360 seconds (6 minutes)
  • Default steps: 30 (flow-matching Euler steps per chunk)
  • CFG scale: Ignored — MiniMax-Music3 uses flow-matching, not classifier-free guidance

Core Principles: The Music Prompt Workflow

VOCAL → STYLE → TEMPO → ARRANGEMENT → PRODUCTION → CONSTRAINTS

This ordering gives you a practical workflow for organizing your music description. It is not a claim about the model's internal training format — it is a way to make sure you cover every dimension that matters.

VOCAL

Purpose: Define voice type, register, timbre, delivery, processing, and harmony.

Effect: Establishes who is singing and how, before genre sets the context.

For instrumental music, explicitly omit vocals: Instrumental, no lead vocal. Name the instrument carrying the lead melodic role.

STYLE

Purpose: Set primary genre, optional secondary influence, and subgenre.

Effect: Gives the model a clear musical target. One strong primary identity works better than a long genre chain.

TEMPO / TIME FEEL

Purpose: Define BPM, key, or rubato duration and dynamic arc.

Effect: Anchors the timing and energy trajectory of the piece.

ARRANGEMENT

Purpose: Describe change over time — what enters, leaves, builds, drops, and breaks down.

Effect: Prevents sparse or static arrangements by specifying section-by-section development.

PRODUCTION

Purpose: Define sonic character, spatial profile, dynamics, and recording aesthetic.

Effect: Polishes the final sound and prevents contradictory audio choices.

CONSTRAINTS

Purpose: State only important exclusions.

Effect: Prevents predictable unwanted conventions without micromanaging every musical decision.

Structured Caption Format

For maximum control, organize your music description into labeled blocks. This matches the Structured Caption format from MiniMax's official music-caption-rewriter skill.

The Schema

GLOBAL METADATA Style: [primary genre + optional secondary influence/subgenre] Tempo / Time feel: [BPM + key OR rubato/free time + duration/arc] Mood: [emotional character + trajectory] VOCAL DETAILS Vocal: [type + register/timbre] Delivery: [phrasing + dynamics] Processing: [dry/doubled/distorted/etc.] Harmony: [harmonies/ad-libs/choir] ARRANGEMENT Intro: [what enters, how it develops] Verse: [instruments, development] Chorus: [full arrangement, key changes] ... PRODUCTION [sonic character + spatial profile + dynamics] CONSTRAINTS [only critical exclusions]

Important: Two separate inputs

The structured caption above goes in the Music Description (prompt) field. The Lyrics field is where you put actual lyrics with structural tags like [Verse], [Chorus]. See the Section Tags in Lyrics section below.

Vocal Details

For vocal-driven music, specify five dimensions: type + register/timbre + delivery + processing + harmony.

Weak vs. Strong Vocal Descriptors

Weak
Female vocals
Stronger
Female mezzo-soprano, warm and breathy, intimate close-mic delivery; opens into a powerful belt in the chorus, subtle vibrato, stacked thirds and octave doubles.

Section Tags in Lyrics

MiniMax-Music3 recognizes structural section tags inside the Lyrics field. These tell the model where to place vocal sections, instrumental breaks, and transitions in the song timeline.

Supported Tags

Place each tag on its own line in the lyrics field:

[Intro]
[Verse]
[Pre-Chorus]
[Chorus]
[Post-Chorus]
[Bridge]
[Instrumental]
[Solo]
[Outro]

Tags to Avoid

Do not invent tags from other models. These are not recognized by MiniMax-Music3:

[Drop]
[Hook]
[Rap Verse]
[Growl]
[Breakdown]

Describe those musical functions in the music description (prompt) instead. For example: Bridge strips to piano and vocal before rebuilding into a heavy half-time final section.

Lyrics Formatting

Correct: tags on their own lines
[Intro] (soft piano, ambient atmosphere) [Verse] Morning light through the window pane Every whisper calls your name [Chorus] We are infinite, we are the stars [Outro] (fade out, piano and strings)

ALICE automatically splits lines that put text on the same line as a tag (e.g. [verse] My heart beats fast becomes two lines), but it's best to format lyrics correctly yourself.

Tempo / Time Feel

For fixed-tempo music, specify BPM and key:

128 BPM, G minor 104 BPM, A minor

When BPM Does Not Fit

For styles with fluid timing, do not force BPM. Use duration, rubato, and texture arc instead:

Slow, rubato, ~7-minute arc, sparse -> gradually dense.

Useful alternatives to BPM:

  • Approximate duration
  • Rubato / free time
  • Groove density
  • Pulse strength
  • Dynamic arc
  • Texture density
  • Rate of harmonic change

Arrangement

Describe change, not just inventory. Tell the model how the piece evolves from section to section.

Weak vs. Strong Arrangement

Weak
Guitar, bass, drums, piano, synth.
Stronger
Verse: fingerpicked guitar + vocal. Pre-chorus: add bass and rising pads. Chorus: full drums, electric guitars and stacked harmonies. Bridge: strip to piano and vocal, then rebuild into final chorus.

Development Patterns

Common arcs for arrangement evolution:

  • Sparse → dense
  • Dry → spacious
  • Acoustic → electronic
  • Solo → full ensemble
  • Half-time → double-time
  • Verse → lift → chorus
  • Build → drop → breakdown → final drop

Production

Use production language to define the sonic target. This is what the final mix should feel like.

Modern hi-fi

Wide stereo, tight low end, crisp transients, controlled dynamics, glossy master.

Raw live-room

Tape saturation, natural dynamics, minimal processing, audible room character.

Lo-fi degraded

Soft transients, tape hiss, degraded texture, restrained high end, unpolished master.

Avoid contradictions

Do not pair contradictory production terms unless the contrast is intentional. Intimate dry vocal with huge distant reverb gives the model competing targets.

Constraints and Exclusions

State only important exclusions — the conventions you need to suppress, not every instrument you don't want.

Effective Exclusions

No trap hi-hats, no obvious autotune, no glossy pop synths. No clean vocals, no polished pop production, no bright synth leads. No pristine mastering, no polished live drums. No drum kit, no electric guitar, no obvious pop structure.

Do not exclude everything. The goal is to prevent important unwanted defaults, not to micromanage every musical decision.

Instrumental Music

For instrumental-forward music, remove vocal instructions and focus on arrangement and texture.

Why name a lead instrument?

ALICE detects empty lyrics and automatically augments the prompt with instrumental version with no vocals, lead instrument: piano. You can override the lead instrument by stating it explicitly in your description.

Dark ambient (instrumental)
Dark ambient with drone and contemporary classical influence. Rubato, ~8-minute gradual arc, cold and isolated. Arrangement: near-silent granular textures -> low drone -> sparse harmonic fragments -> increasing textural density -> broad sustained climax -> gradual dissolution. Production: huge stereo field, very long reverberation, deep sub frequencies, soft transients, subtle tape degradation. Constraints: instrumental only, no percussion, no obvious rhythmic pulse, no lead melody.

Best Practices

Specify What Matters Most

Do not treat vocal specificity as universally more important than genre. Use vocal specificity for vocal-driven music. Use genre specificity for strongly genre-coded music. Use arrangement/texture specificity for instrumental-forward music. Use both genre and vocal identity when the genre encodes the vocal style.

Describe Change, Not Just Inventory

Prefer Verse sparse -> chorus full -> bridge stripped -> final chorus expanded. over Guitar, bass, drums, piano, synth, strings, pads, percussion...

Prefer Clear Genre Relationships

Prefer Alternative rock with post-punk elements. over Rock + pop + metal + electronic + indie + cinematic.

Match Production to Style

Production terms strongly change the sonic target:

  • pristine / hi-fi / polished
  • raw / live / saturated / lo-fi

Avoid combinations such as Intimate dry vocal with huge distant reverb unless the contrast is intentional.

Prompt Length Trade-offs

Use shorter prompts when: Use longer prompts when:
Genre identity is conventional You need explicit sectional development
Arrangement is straightforward Arrangement is unusually dense
You want fewer competing instructions Several transitions matter
You are rapidly iterating Instrument roles need to be controlled
Model tends to produce overly sparse arrangements You want a specific dynamic or textural arc

Prevent Unwanted Conventions

State only the conventions you need to suppress, not everything. No trap hi-hats, no obvious autotune, no glossy pop synths. is targeted. No trap hi-hats, no autotune, no synths, no drums, no bass, no guitar, no piano, no strings... is micromanagement.

Final Checklist Before Generation

  • Music description specifies genre, mood, and vocal identity
  • Tempo/time feel or duration is defined
  • Arrangement describes change between sections
  • Production terms match the style (not contradictory)
  • Only critical exclusions are stated
  • Lyrics (if any) use supported section tags on their own lines
  • Instrumental tracks explicitly say "no vocals" + name lead instrument
